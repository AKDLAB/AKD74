Focus area

Bio and Chemo Sensors

A biosensor couples a biological recognition element — an antibody, enzyme, bacteriophage, aptamer or glycan — to a transducer that converts the binding event into a measurable signal. We build these interfaces on nanomaterials such as graphene and graphene quantum dots, MoS2, carbon nanotubes and metal–organic frameworks, whose large surface area, conductivity or luminescence amplify the signal. Targets range from clinical biomarkers to foodborne and waterborne pathogens and food toxins.

How a nanomaterial biosensor turns binding into a signal

  1. 1

    Immobilize

    Receptors are anchored on the nanomaterial, commonly by carbodiimide (EDC/NHS) coupling of surface carboxyl groups to receptor amines:

    R–COOH + H2N–R′ → R–CO–NH–R′ + H2O

    MOFs can also encapsulate enzymes, shielding them from denaturation.

  2. 2

    Recognize

    The target binds selectively to its receptor, governed by the dissociation constant:

    Ab + Ag ⇌ Ab·Ag
    KD = [Ab][Ag] / [Ab·Ag]

    Bacteriophages bind their host bacteria through strain-specific receptor proteins.

  3. 3

    Transduce

    Binding changes an interfacial property. In impedimetric sensors the captured layer blocks the [Fe(CN)6]3−/4− redox probe, raising the charge-transfer resistance:

    ΔRct = Rct(bound) − Rct(blank)

    Optical sensors read fluorescence quenching or a shift in surface plasmon resonance instead.

  4. 4

    Quantify

    A calibration curve over the linear range gives sensitivity and the limit of detection:

    LOD = 3σblank / S

    where S is the calibration slope. Sensors are then validated in real samples such as serum, milk and water.

Our approaches

Electrochemical immunosensors

Label-free impedimetric and conductometric immunosensors built on graphene quantum dots, amine-functionalized graphene, gold nanoparticle–reduced graphene oxide and TCNQ-doped Cu-MOF films, for cardiac markers (troponin I, myoglobin) and prostate-specific antigen.

Bacteriophage biosensors

Phages as robust, strain-specific receptors on MOF and graphene surfaces, giving optical and impedimetric detection of Staphylococcus aureus and S. arlettae.

Luminescent & fibre-optic sensors

Water-dispersible terbium MOFs for dipicolinic acid (an anthrax spore biomarker), and optical-fibre surface plasmon resonance sensors modified with 2D transition-metal dichalcogenides for proteins such as BSA, ferritin and IgG.

Enzyme–MOF bioconjugates

Enzymes immobilized in or on MOFs for stability and reuse, such as organophosphate hydrolase on UiO-66-NH2 for methyl parathion, and glucose oxidase on long-period fibre gratings for glucose.

Food safety

Carbon quantum dot lateral-flow immunoassays for aflatoxin M1 in milk, MoS2 quantum dot/MOF and MOF/MXene electrochemical sensors for aflatoxins, and a UiO-66-NH2 fluorescent aptasensor for zearalenone in cereals.

Pathogen detection

MOF/polyaniline and MIL-53/PEDOT electrodes and terbium-MOF optical probes for E. coli, and glyco-conjugated MOFs for fluorescent detection of bacteria.

Target analytes

  • Cardiac biomarkers (troponin I, myoglobin)
  • Prostate-specific antigen
  • S. aureus, E. coli and other pathogens
  • Anthrax biomarker (dipicolinic acid)
  • Mycotoxins (aflatoxins, zearalenone)
  • Glucose and proteins

Focus area

Sensors for Environmental Analytes

Pollutants in air and water — pesticides, heavy-metal ions, toxic gases and volatile organic compounds — must be measured at trace levels, in the field, and in complex matrices. We engineer the sensing layer at the molecular level: porous MOFs pre-concentrate the analyte inside their pores, while conductive or luminescent partners such as graphene, polyaniline, PEDOT:PSS and MXenes convert that uptake into an electrical or optical readout.

From a trace pollutant to a readable signal

  1. 1

    Pre-concentrate

    Analyte molecules adsorb into MOF pores or onto open metal sites, raising their local concentration at the sensing surface far above that in the surrounding air or water.

  2. 2

    Gas: resistance change

    Adsorbed gas donates or withdraws electrons, changing the film's carrier density. On p-type films, electron-donating NH3 fills holes and raises resistance:

    Response (%) = (Rgas − Rair) / Rair × 100

    Several of our composites, such as ZIF-67/rGO for ammonia and Zr-MOF/PEDOT:PSS for toluene, work at room temperature with no heater.

  3. 3

    Metals & organics

    Heavy metals are measured by anodic stripping voltammetry: deposit, then re-oxidize to a current peak proportional to concentration:

    M2+ + 2e− → M0  →  M0 → M2+ + 2e−

    Nitroaromatics quench MOF luminescence (Stern–Volmer):

    I0 / I = 1 + KSV[Q]

  4. 4

    Deploy

    Sensing films are printed onto low-cost platforms, such as inkjet-printed MOF patterns and screen-printed electrodes, and tested for selectivity against interferents and in spiked real samples.

Our approaches

Chemoresistive gas sensors

Room-temperature ammonia sensing with ZIF-67/reduced graphene oxide, Cu-MOF–graphene–polyaniline and nanostructured polyaniline films; toluene sensing with Zr-MOF/PEDOT:PSS; and low-cost inkjet-printed MOF patterns.

Electrochemical heavy-metal sensors

Anodic stripping voltammetry on amine-functionalized Fe-MOF for cadmium, and on ZIF-67/rGO for simultaneous detection of lead and cadmium.

Pesticide sensors

Impedimetric parathion sensors from nano-MOF films on amine-functionalized ITO, antibody-functionalized Cu-MOF films for atrazine, and luminescent MOF-5 for nitro-containing organophosphates.

Luminescent MOF probes

Turn-off detection of nitroaromatics such as nitrobenzene and trinitrotoluene, and of formaldehyde in water, using Zr- and lanthanide-based frameworks.

Optical heavy-metal probes

Surface-functionalized carbon dots for dual-mode detection of Pb2+, building on our reviews of fluorescent and noble-metal colorimetric sensors for lead.

Antibiotic residues

A Cu-MOF modified electrosensor for ciprofloxacin and a Zr-MOF opto-electrochemical sensor for nitrofurazone.

  • Toluene & volatile organic compounds
  • Formaldehyde
  • Hydrogen sulfide
  • Pb2+, Cd2+, arsenic
  • Organophosphates & atrazine
  • Nitroaromatic explosives
  • Antibiotic residues

Focus area

Energy Applications of Nanomaterials

Storing and converting energy efficiently depends on electrodes that combine a large electroactive surface, fast ion transport and good electrical conductivity. We use metal–organic frameworks as tunable templates: directly, in conductive composites, or as sacrificial precursors that convert into porous metal oxide, sulfide or phosphide/carbon hybrids. We pair these electrodes with redox-additive electrolytes to raise energy density, and extend the same materials to batteries and solar cells.

How a MOF-derived supercapacitor electrode stores charge

  1. 1

    Template

    A framework such as ZIF-67, ZIF-8, UiO-66 or a Ni-MOF is grown with defined metal nodes, pore size and particle shape, including core–shell architectures.

  2. 2

    Convert

    Controlled pyrolysis, sulfidation or phosphidation turns the MOF into a porous hybrid (ZrO2/C, Co3S4, ZnS/C, nickel phosphide/C) that keeps the parent's porosity while gaining conductivity.

  3. 3

    Store

    Charge is held by double-layer adsorption and by fast surface redox, e.g. for nickel sites in alkaline electrolyte:

    Ni(OH)2 + OH− ⇌ NiOOH + H2O + e−

    We separate surface-capacitive and diffusion-controlled contributions (Dunn's method):

    i(V) = k1ν + k2ν1/2

  4. 4

    Deliver

    Energy grows with the square of the cell voltage, so widening the stable window (our 1.8 V symmetric and 2 V flexible solid-state devices) pays off quickly:

    E = ½ C V2
    Pmax = V2 / 4Rs

Our approaches

MOF-derived electrodes

ZrO2/carbon from UiO-66, Co3S4 hollow microspheres from ZIF-67, TiO2–carbon, ZnS–carbon from ZIF-8, and nickel phosphide/carbon from Ni-MOF.

Conductive MOF composites

ZIF-67/rGO, nickel-intercalated ZIF-67/rGO, ZIF-67/PEDOT and TCNQ-doped Cu-MOF electrodes for all-solid-state symmetric supercapacitors, often with redox-additive electrolytes.

Core–shell & surface-mounted MOFs

UiO-66@ZIF-8 and core–nanoshell ZIF architectures for asymmetric supercapacitors with high energy density, and surface-mounted MOF films for storage and conversion.

Waste-derived carbons

Highly porous activated carbons from neem leaves, waste office paper and other bio-waste, including waste-paper carbon combined with ZIF-8 crystals.

Batteries

MOFs and their derivatives as lithium-ion battery anodes, and ZnS/carbon composites for flexible sodium-ion hybrid capacitors.

Solar energy conversion

Quantum-dot/MOF photoanodes (CdTe/Eu-MOF, CdTe/NTU-9) for quantum-dot-sensitized solar cells, perovskite platforms, and photocatalytic hydrogen production from H2S.

Devices

  • Symmetric & asymmetric supercapacitors
  • Flexible solid-state capacitors
  • Sodium-ion hybrid capacitors
  • Lithium-ion battery anodes
  • Quantum-dot-sensitized solar cells
  • Photocatalytic hydrogen production

Focus area

Recovery from E-waste & Low-grade Sources

Spent batteries, electronic scrap, industrial effluents and low-grade ores still hold valuable metals: zinc, cobalt, nickel, copper, indium, chromium, titanium and the rare earths. Recovering them eases pressure on primary mining and keeps toxic metals out of the environment. Our work combines hydrometallurgy (leaching followed by selective solvent extraction) with upcycling the recovered metals directly into nanomaterials and MOFs.

From waste to a high-purity product

  1. 1

    Leach

    Mineral acids, or greener organic acids for lithium-ion battery cathodes, dissolve metals from the waste matrix into solution:

    MO + 2H+ → M2+ + H2O

  2. 2

    Extract

    A selective extractant in an organic diluent pulls the target ion across the liquid–liquid interface, by solvation (Cyanex 923) or cation exchange (Cyanex 272, ACORGA M5640):

    M2+(aq) + 2HA(org) ⇌ MA2(org) + 2H+(aq)

    D = [M]org / [M]aq    β = DA / DB

  3. 3

    Strip

    The loaded organic phase is contacted with a stripping solution that reverses the equilibrium, giving a concentrated, purified metal solution and regenerating the extractant for reuse.

  4. 4

    Upcycle

    The recovered metal becomes a product: high-purity ZnO nanoparticles from spent alkaline batteries, or zinc used directly as the metal source for MOF synthesis.

Our approaches

Spent battery recycling

Chemical routes to high-quality ZnO nanoparticles from spent Zn–MnO2 alkaline batteries, and eco-friendly organic-acid leaching of metals from waste lithium-ion batteries.

Waste-to-MOF upcycling

Zinc recovered from alkaline battery waste used directly as the metal precursor for metal–organic framework synthesis, turning waste into functional materials.

Solvent extraction of critical metals

Cyanex 923-based extraction and separation of indium, cadmium, 3d transition-metal ions, and tervalent lanthanides and yttrium.

Rare earths & actinides

Separation and recovery of uranium, thorium and lanthanides from monazite, and analysis of global rare-earth demand and strategies for green mining.

Industrial effluents & residues

Fe(III) and Cr(III) from tannery filtrates, chromium and nickel from industrial waste, Ti(IV) from ilmenite and red mud, and copper from zinc leach liquors.

Secondary sources

Cobalt, nickel and copper from sea nodules with alkylphosphine extractants, and cobalt and nickel from spent catalysts.

Focus area

Environmental Remediation & Wastewater Treatment

Industrial and domestic effluents carry contaminants that conventional treatment removes poorly: synthetic dyes, pharmaceuticals, organophosphate pesticides, heavy-metal ions, and pathogenic bacteria. We design porous metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) and their composites that first capture these species and then destroy or recover them, aiming for complete mineralization rather than moving the pollution from water into sludge.

How a MOF photocatalyst cleans water

  1. 1

    Capture

    Pollutant molecules diffuse into the framework's pores and bind through electrostatic attraction, π–π stacking, hydrogen bonding, or coordination to open metal sites.

  2. 2

    Activate

    Visible light excites the framework or a sensitizing quantum dot, creating separated charges:

    hν ≥ Eg → e−CB + h+VB

    Heterojunctions slow electron–hole recombination.

  3. 3

    Degrade

    The charges generate reactive oxygen species that attack the pollutant:

    e− + O2 → •O2−
    h+ + H2O → •OH + H+

    In iron-based MOFs, Fe3+/Fe2+ cycling also activates H2O2 (photo-Fenton):

    Fe2+ + H2O2 → Fe3+ + •OH + OH−

  4. 4

    Recover

    Organic pollutants are mineralized toward CO2 and H2O. Magnetic composites are pulled out with an external magnet and reused over repeated cycles.

Our approaches

Adsorptive removal

Amine- and thiol-functionalized frameworks such as magnetic NH2-MIL-101(Fe) and cysteine-modified Al-MOFs, whose high surface area and tunable pore chemistry give strong, selective uptake. Uptake is characterized through equilibrium isotherms (Langmuir, Freundlich), kinetic models (pseudo-first- and pseudo-second-order), and thermodynamic parameters (ΔG°, ΔH°, ΔS°).

Photocatalytic degradation

Quantum-dot/MOF and polymer/quantum-dot nanocomposites that harvest visible light and separate photogenerated charges efficiently, degrading persistent dyes such as rhodamine 6G. Iron-based frameworks extend this to photo-Fenton oxidation.

Detect-and-remove platforms

Dual-function materials that report a contaminant and remove it in the same step, such as a cysteine-functionalized Al-MOF for simultaneous chemosensing and removal of Cu2+, and MOF/MXene hybrids that combine conductivity with high adsorption capacity.

Pathogen removal

Recyclable magnetic mesoporous MIL-125(Ti) composites that capture waterborne pathogens and are separated magnetically for reuse.

Low-cost sorbents

Agro-waste derived materials such as sugarcane bagasse ash for cost-effective removal of methylene blue from dye effluents, under both UV and dark conditions.

Current direction: MOF membranes

MOF composite membranes that pair size- and charge-selective separation with photocatalytic self-cleaning to resist fouling, and how water chemistry (pH, ionic strength, natural organic matter) governs their performance.

Target contaminants

  • Synthetic dyes (rhodamine 6G, methylene blue, bromothymol blue)
  • Emerging contaminants & pharmaceuticals
  • Organophosphate pesticides
  • Heavy-metal ions (Cu2+)
  • Waterborne pathogens
  • Radionuclides
